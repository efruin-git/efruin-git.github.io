Chapter 5

Geisinger started their reengineering efforts in cardiology care.  Their new top goal was “fundamental innovation and continuous improvement in how we provided care” (page 51).

They started with the assumption that “clinical leadership would likely be the most important lever in getting key professionals to change how they gave care.  And we assumed that the best clinical leaders were most likely in the service lines or departments already producing the best results” (page 51).

The Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council (PHC4) was an independent state agency.  Geisinger considered them the best source for quality outcome metrics to use as a benchmark against other Pennsylvania hospitals.  The metrics focused on quality improvement and cost containment.

Geisinger leaders “wanted to pick a high-volume, high-cost, high-visibility hospital-associated episode of care at the top of the PHC4 reports,” then redesign care from “diagnosis through post-acute-care rehabilitation (page 52).  They chose elective Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG) surgery and interventional cardiology for stent placement.

The ProvenCare Acute process started in 2004-2005, and the Geisinger team could not find one example of another hospital who had used evidence-based practices to establish a complete standardized care pathway.  (find and list examples of innovative treatments from the 70s, 80s, and 90s to explain that Geisinger’s claims of innovation may be a little exaggerated)

Geisinger Community Practice Service Line (for ambulatory care) – multidisciplinary service line, geographically dispersed, 55 sites providing care across Pennsylvania.  “It primarily was responsible for the care of nearly 30,000 type 2 diabetes patients,” was well run, and was “Geisinger’s most innovative care delivery group” (page 54).

New programs named ProvenCare Heart and ProvenCare Chronic

Single price warranty for entire episode of care and any preventable complications that ensued.

Geisinger rolled out their innovative pricing and reimbursement structures first in their Medicare managed care plans, then in their commercial plans, and lastly in their Medicaid managed care plans (page 56).  They used internal transfer pricing to reimburse physicians for improved patient outcomes and lower cost.  They rewarded physicians for fewer complications, decreases in unnecessary or wasteful procedures like test duplications, shorter lengths of stay, and decreased readmissions.  20 percent  of physician compensation became linked to patient care benefits.

Physicians were motivated the most by “professional pride of purpose” (mentioned throughout the book), which resulted in better long-term patient outcomes.

Geisinger had early successes in 2004-2008 that were enhanced by positive attention in medical journals and mainstream media.  It motivated their staff and their leaders.  To maintain motivation, leaders focused on the hearts, minds, and wallets of their staff (page 64).

A lesson learned in the early years of ProvenCare about messaging was to “keep refining, but repeating, internal and external narratives” (page 61).

Chapter 6 – Enabling Change

Geisinger used combined clinical, operational, and financial leadership teams.  If the clinical leaders did not agree to equal input from the other leaders, they were replaced (page 65).

The increasing complexity of the healthcare environment caused many small crises for leaders to worry about.

Advances in communication technology compounded the problem, as thought was supplanted by action, thoughtful action was replaced by motion, and discussion was replaced by transaction.  Human interactions were interrupted by the telephone; the telephone was replaced by e-mail, then text messaging and tweeting.  Writing with punctuation disappeared, and thinking was diluted by multitasking.  Thinking and discussion with others was replaced by chaotic social media.  It was imperative to slow this decline into chaotic activity, which we did through intentional structural design. (page 66)

Geisinger created a position for an executive vice president for strategy and strategic program development.  The new leader “became the most important colleague of the CEO and the chief medical officer (CMO)” (page 66).

They created a department called Geisinger Innovations that focused exclusively on innovation efforts.  Leaders included nurses representing both the provider and payer sides of the organization, IT experts, and health economists (page 67).

Innovation focus, led by dyad or triad of clinical, operational, and financial leaders – “The objective was to obtain baseline data regarding indications for a particular procedure and to inventory the occurrence of unjustified variation in each aspect of routine practice for an episode of care.  The leaders of the caregiving disciplines or interdisciplinary service lines simply needed to lead the effort to inventory the baseline data and then to help socialize whatever became the default best practice” (page 68).

Geisinger Transformation was created to deal with cost issues.  It’s mission was to “increase efficiency across our system and extract 15 to 20 percent of our cost structure over three years” (page 69).

“value reengineering continues to be dependent upon either embedding innovations into transactional EHRs or bolting innovation onto functional applications” (page 70).

Geisinger focused on scaling and generalizing their care and products using three platforms.  They scaled their payer/provider capabilities into areas in Pennsylvania and outside Pennsylvania where they had fewer customers or used nonemployed caregiving partners in their hospitals.  They scaled Geisinger insurance into new states – locations where the hospital systems were not owned by Geisinger.  They created xG Health Solutions consulting group as a third scaling platform and for-profit spin-off. (page 71)

Geisinger had some early successes in scaling to an organization in Virginia and another in rural Illinois and Wisconsin.  Overall their scaling efforts had mixed results.  In some organizations they saw decreases in hospitalizations, readmissions, and ED visits, but the improvements would fade if hospital leadership changed and the new leaders were not as committed to value-based care.  If volume of services decreased from better quality care, hospital leaders in some organizations would increase unit prices.  They also ran into challenges scaling their insurance products, having to compete against dominant local payers.

There were several practices, though, that xG Health Solutions and Geisinger leaders determined were successful within Geisinger and truly scalable to other systems.  They could implement improvements to the EHR and add bolt-on software, improve data warehousing and analytics, establish consensus based care pathways integrated into clinical work flows, ensure providers practice at the top of their license, use care management professionals, and provide training and financial incentives for innovation and strategic goals. (page 75)


Chapter 7 – ProvenCare Acute: Taking It to the Next Step

P 90 – “Most of the analysis, either evidence-based or consensus-based, of what should be done every time for every patient from the diagnosis of coronary artery disease through rehabilitation had already been processed by the cardiology community and the cardiac surgery disciplines.  What had never been done was a systematic care pathway reprogramming, enabling all of the best practices to be the default for every patient who was diagnosed with coronary artery disease and proposed to be a candidate for CABG.  So ProvenCare Acute really began as a sociology experiment.”

P 81 – “For hospitalized patient interventions, the recovery room is one of the most critical sites.  Determinants of good outcome following most major surgeries are prevention of infection and blood clots that migrate from the leg veins to the lungs.”  Standardized procedures for antibiotics and other elements of care can make a big difference in patient outcomes.  A major element of ProvenCare pathways is reducing variation in care.

P 82, 83 – 

ProvenCare is a formalized process initiated to:
-	document appropriateness of care
-	establish evidence- or consensus-based best practices for all high-frequency services
-	reliably deliver default best practices by redesign of complex clinical systems that embeds new behaviors into everyday patient flow
-	activate patients and their families and engage them in the care processes as symmetrical partners with caregivers
-	provide a packaged “warrant” that transfers the risk for the financial effects of preventable complications to the caregiver.

P 86, 87 – Process redesign starts with eliminating unnecessary tests and procedures.  “Throughout the effort, administrative and clinical commitment to attacking unnecessary, redundant, or hurtful care became almost a religious belief in striving for higher quality and has remained the most important success factor” (page 87).  

ProvenCare pathways prioritize work that can be performed with technology.  Whenever possible, work is assigned to nonphysician staff so physicians have more time for direct patient care.

P 94 – The ProvenCare Heart CABG program reduced in-hospital mortality, complications, stroke, re-intubation, blood product usage, infections, and other quality outcomes.  Financially, ProvenCare CABG increased contribution margin 17.6% and inpatient profit per case about $2,000.  Geisinger Insurance also benefitted from the redesigned care.  They paid out 4.8% less per case.

P 94 – Hip surgery was the second ProvenCare Acute program.  It produced successful quality outcomes including reductions in length of stay, complications, readmissions, and blood clots.  Hospital-based financial outcomes for the hip surgery program improved to a similar degree as ProvenCare CABG.

Chapter 8

P 110 – ProvenCare Chronic was initiated with a program to improve diabetes treatment.  As with ProvenCare Acute, evidence-based guidelines played a major role in determining treatment protocols.  Geisinger established a set of 14 diabetes related measures to standardize care.  The measures include Hgb and LDL lab results, blood pressure readings, foot and eye exams, medications, immunizations, and smoking status.  All 14 measures are required for each patient.

ProvenCare Chronic care pathways “eliminate, automate, delegate, incorporate, and activate” (page 110).  The EHR is programmed to present best practice alerts and order smart sets.  Work is delegated to trained nonphysician staff whenever possible.  Patient letters and emails are used in an attempt to fully engage the patient in their own treatment and health outcomes.

The ProvenCare Chronic Diabetes program resulted in cost of care savings around 15% after four years and lesser savings in the first four years.

P 114 – Regarding the success of ProvenCare Chronic programs: “Most important was the commitment of PCPs to consider everything known in the literature about prevention as a “must-do” for their patients, with rational compartmentalization regarding what was appropriate for various age groups, lifestyles, and behaviors.”

P 115 – “Hemoglobin A1C at goal increased 45 percent over a seven-year period for a study population of 25,000 people, and LDL at goal increased 18 percent over the same period in the same population, even after establishing more strenuous goals.  Most important, there were also reduced rates of stroke, myocardial infarction, and retinopathy in the same population.”

P 116 – “The enabling technology and the new pathway had to be easier than what was done previously, because change would not occur if the pathways were more complex than the ones already in use.”

P 122 – Using claims data from Geisinger Health Plan and other sources, researchers found a 7% cost savings for diabetes patients treated using the ProvenCare Chronic pathways over 2006-2013 compared to patients treated without ProvenCare Chronic pathways.  Decreased inpatient facility usage was the biggest cost savings element.


Chapter 9 – ProvenHealth Navigator: Geisinger’s Advanced Medical Home

P 127 – Geisinger embeds nurse care managers in the primary care office.  They are employed by the insurance company.  They serve as a “concierge caregiver for the sickest patients” (page 127).  Patients with chronic conditions use the most resources.  Having a dedicated care manager helps the care team cut down on unnecessary and wasteful care.  It also helps engage the patient and the patient’s family in the patient’s health journey.  More effort and accountability from the patient and their family makes a big difference in outcomes.

The care managers help manage chronic illness, ensure the patients are safe at home, help patients take medications as prescribed, assist in obtaining transportation for appointments, and ensure patients receive recommended vaccinations for flu, pneumonia, and shingles.

ProvenHealth Navigator proved successful at reducing inpatient hospital admissions and readmissions.  ProvenHealth Navigator was implemented as part of the ProvenCare Chronic Diabetes program.  The results for the diabetes patient population can also be credited to ProvenHealth Navigator contributions.

P 139 – “Care managers are given variable caseloads, with approximately 300 at-risk patients and 125 to 150 complex chronic disease aggregate patients assigned to each care manager.

Although employed by our insurance company, the care managers work as members of the community practice team, providing information from the insurance company that is modified for immediate use by the entire team.”

Chapter 11 – ProvenCare Biologics

Geisinger expanded its bundled best-practice pathways to include medication prescribing and administration.  They found there were sub-populations of common conditions that were being treated with unnecessarily expensive medications, when a different medication would work just as effectively.  For example, more than 20 percent of chronic renal failure patients with anemia could be treated with oral iron, intravenous iron dextran, or iron sucrose at a fraction of the cost of the typical medications – EPO biologicals.  Some patients, especially older patients, have higher risk of an adverse reaction to EPO treatment and fare better with an iron medication, so the iron alternatives are not only cheaper, but safer for some patients as well.  ProvenCare Biologics introduced a more stringent, standardized set of indications for treatment, resulting in decreased cost and better outcomes.

Another improvement established with the new care pathway was changing the location of medication administration from the nephrologist’s office (in the case of renal failure patients) to a central, less costly location for care, to be administered by trained staff following best-practice guidelines.  The new protocols for medication administration resulted in less variable, less costly, and improved, processes and outcomes.

ProvenCare Biologics was also implemented for patients with cancer, psoriasis, hepatitis c, and multiple sclerosis.

Background

ProvenCare is a book about healthcare quality improvement at Geisinger Hospital in Pennsylvania, written by Glenn D. Steele Jr., MD, president and CEO from 2001 to 2015 and current CEO David T. Feinberg, MD, who started in May 2015.  The quality improvement initiatives documented in the book started in 2004-2005 after a failed merger with Penn State Hershey Medical Center that lasted three years.  Geisinger was rebuilding and looking for ways to improve its finances and outcomes.

The authors tout the many advances Geisinger made in patient care, while being careful to acknowledge the leaders found themselves in a situation ripe for innovation.  The majority of Geisinger’s patients lived in central Pennsylvania and were “older, sicker, and poorer than just about any demography in the United States outside the Deep South” (page 7).  The patients had traditional values and were trusting of Geisinger leaders and employees.  The patient population was also very stable.  

Geisinger is a health system and also an insurer.  Geisinger Health Plan was started in the 1970s.  Partnership between the insurer and the hospital helped in implementing value-based care initiatives.  Reimbursement rates were structured so providers would be rewarded for more efficient care.

Geisinger implemented their EPIC EHR in ambulatory care sites in 1995 and now Geisinger uses EPIC and Cerner across their facilities.  The increased adoption of EHR systems also made innovation possible.  The EHR was used to implement best practice alerts, workflow programming, automate processes, store clinical information, and as a patient portal so patients could communicate with providers and review their clinical and lab results.
