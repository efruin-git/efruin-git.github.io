*ProvenCare: How to Deliver Value-Based Healthcare the Geisinger Way*, By Glenn D. Steele Jr., MD and David T. Feinberg, MD

## ProvenCare Book Summary and Review

### Background

ProvenCare is a book about healthcare quality improvement at Geisinger Hospital in Pennsylvania, written by Glenn D. Steele Jr., MD, president and CEO from 2001 to 2015 and current CEO David T. Feinberg, MD, who started in May 2015.  The quality improvement initiatives documented in the book started in 2004-2005 after a failed merger with Penn State Hershey Medical Center that lasted three years.  Geisinger was rebuilding and looking for ways to improve its finances and outcomes.

The authors tout the many advances Geisinger made in patient care, while being careful to acknowledge the leaders found themselves in a situation ripe for innovation.  The majority of Geisinger’s patients lived in central Pennsylvania and were “older, sicker, and poorer than just about any demography in the United States outside the Deep South” (ProvenCare, p 7).  The patients had traditional values and were trusting of Geisinger leaders and employees.  The patient population was also very stable.  

Geisinger is a health system and also an insurer.  Geisinger Health Plan was started in the 1970s.  Partnership between the insurer and the hospital helped in implementing value-based care initiatives.  Reimbursement rates were structured so providers would be rewarded for more efficient care.

Geisinger implemented their EPIC EHR in ambulatory care sites in 1995 and now Geisinger uses EPIC and Cerner across their facilities.  The increased adoption of EHR systems also made innovation possible.  The EHR was used to implement best practice alerts, workflow programming, automate processes, store clinical information, and as a patient portal so patients could communicate with providers and review their clinical and lab results.

### Motivation and Quality Improvement Fundamentals

Dr. Steele was motivated by an experience from when he was in medical school.  His father underwent heart surgery.  His father had a Coronary Artery Bypass Graft and the family was charged for two operations – one was the CABG surgery and the second was a procedure to repair complications of the CABG surgery.  That got Dr. Steele thinking about how quality, cost, and patient experience could be improved.

He was also motivated by a couple meaningful research articles from the early 2000s.  A RAND study published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* found that 45 percent of care was “suboptimal, split evenly between too much, too little, and the wrong care” (p 20).  The other research was performed by Arnold Milstein, cofounder of the Leapfrog Group and the Consumer-Purchaser Disclosure Project.  Milstein found there was “no relationship between the cost of care and quality-of-care outcomes in the United States” (p 20).  The observations of Dr. Steele and his team supported the research.  They found that patients with the worst outcomes often had the highest cost.  They believed there was tremendous opportunity for more efficient, effective, and patient-centered care.

Physician, administrative, and financial leaders worked together to improve care at Geisinger, with leaders from the hospital system and the insurance company integrated on some teams.  By sharing data from the payer and provider sides of the organization, they were able to not only uncover financial opportunities, but to pinpoint patients and conditions with the most opportunity for improvement.

Geisinger leaders focused their rebuilding efforts on increasing access, creating more specialty programs and demand (the number of specialty programs in central and northeast Pennsylvania was low), opening the patient payer mix to other insurance companies, providing the right incentives for physicians, and improving the research and education functions.

The authors acknowledge compensation was a significant element in achieving the goals and changes the leaders desired.  20 percent of compensation for both system leaders and clinicians was linked to strategic goals in an effort to replace the traditional fee-for-service model.  They authors explain, “How we paid our employees was the engine ensuring that the strategic plan would be aligned and affirmed throughout the organization” (p 33).

The big results of the innovation efforts were bundled best-practice pathways of care they called ProvenCare.  The authors define ProvenCare as a formalized process initiated to (p 82, 83):
-	document appropriateness of care
-	establish evidence- or consensus-based best practices for all high-frequency services
-	reliably deliver default best practices by redesign of complex clinical systems that embeds new behaviors into everyday patient flow
-	activate patients and their families and engage them in the care processes as symmetrical partners with caregivers
-	provide a packaged “warranty” that transfers the risk for the financial effects of preventable complications to the caregiver

### ProvenCare Acute

Geisinger started their reengineering efforts in cardiology care.  They chose cardiology care based on a few assumptions.  They believed clinical leaders would play the most important role in care redesign, would be fundamentally important in influencing others to adopt the changes, and that the best clinical leaders were already part of service lines and departments with strong results.

The leaders used quality outcome measures from the Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council (PHC4) to gauge their improvement efforts.  PHC4 was an independent state agency.  Geisinger considered PHC4 metrics the best benchmark against other Pennsylvania hospitals.  PHC4 metrics focused on quality improvement and cost containment.  

Geisinger leaders chose a “high-volume, high-cost, high-visibility hospital-associated episode of care at the top of the PHC4 reports” (p 52) to use for their first reengineering effort - elective Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG) surgery.  They wanted to redesign care from diagnosis through rehabilitation.  

They attempted to eliminate unnecessary care and variation in care by implementing standardized evidence-based best practices.  The ProvenCare Acute CABG program reduced in-hospital mortality, complications, stroke, re-intubation, blood product usage, and infections.  Financially, ProvenCare CABG increased contribution margin 17.6% and inpatient profit per case about $2,000.  Geisinger Insurance also benefitted from the redesigned care.  They paid out 4.8% less per case.

Hip surgery was the second ProvenCare Acute program.  It produced successful quality outcomes including reductions in length of stay, complications, readmissions, and blood clots.  Hospital-based financial outcomes for the hip surgery program improved to a similar degree as ProvenCare Acute CABG.

ProvenCare Acute pathways have been expanded to several clinical areas including:
-	Behavioral health
-	Pulmonology
-	Infectious Diseases
-	Cancer
-	Gastroenterology
-	OB/GYN
-	Neurology
-	Orthopedics

### ProvenCare Chronic Diabetes

ProvenCare Chronic was created to address chronic diseases and began with a program to improve diabetes treatment.  As with ProvenCare Acute, evidence-based guidelines played a major role in determining treatment protocols.  Geisinger established a set of 14 diabetes related measures to standardize care.  The measures included Hgb and LDL lab results, blood pressure readings, foot and eye exams, medications, immunizations, and smoking status.  All 14 measures were required for each patient.

From a hospital perspective, the ProvenCare Chronic Diabetes program resulted in cost of care savings around 15% after four years and lesser savings in the first four years.  There were many positive health outcomes from the program.  “Hemoglobin A1C at goal increased 45 percent over a seven-year period for a study population of 25,000 people, and LDL at goal increased 18 percent over the same period in the same population, even after establishing more strenuous goals.  Most important, there were also reduced rates of stroke, myocardial infarction, and retinopathy in the same population” (p 115).

If the hospital cost of care savings from the ProvenCare Chronic Diabetes program are averaged to an annual amount, they show a similar savings rate to that found by researchers using claims data from Geisinger Health Plan and other sources from 2006-2013.  Researchers found a 7% cost savings for diabetes patients treated using ProvenCare Chronic pathways over 2006-2013 compared to patients treated without ProvenCare Chronic pathways.  Decreased inpatient facility usage was the biggest cost savings element cited in the study, as care was decentralized and provided more frequently in clinics and ambulatory settings.

Other chronic diseases addressed with ProvenCare Chronic programs include congestive heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.  

### Key Elements Leading to Success of ProvenCare Programs

There were a number of fundamental elements that led to improved outcomes from care redesign.  Geisinger leaders created an innovation department where employees focused solely on improving care, they aligned communication and incentives with their strategic goals, they embedded processes into EHR workflows and added bolt-on software, they established consensus-based care pathways, they ensured providers practice at the top of their license, and they used care management professionals to coordinate care for high cost patients.  They were motivated by “professional pride of purpose” and developed an almost religious belief in striving for higher quality through eliminating unnecessary, redundant, or hurtful care (p 87).

Geisinger created innovative pricing and reimbursement structures.  They rolled out their reimbursement structures first in their Medicare managed care plans, then in their commercial plans, and lastly in their Medicaid managed care plans.  They used internal transfer pricing to reimburse physicians for improved patient outcomes and lower cost.  They rewarded physicians for fewer complications, shorter lengths of stay, decreased readmissions, and decreases in unnecessary or wasteful procedures like test duplications.  20 percent of physician compensation was linked to patient care benefits.

Another financial innovation was made possible by having both the hospital system and insurance company in one organization – the “warranty” for episodes of care.  The authors explain, “For 10 ProvenCare acute and chronic services, we charge a flat fee that in essence includes a commitment or “warranty” to cover complications and readmissions for 90 days” (p 2).  The reimbursement structure for clinicians along with the warranty created incentives for care teams to provide only necessary care, and to use best practices to prevent complications and readmissions.

Multidisciplinary teams led by triads of clinical, financial, and administrative leaders were involved in creating the new care pathways.  Patient care was decentralized and provided in clinics and physician offices when possible.  Specialists partnered with primary care providers to teach best practices and monitor patient progress.  Specialists were freed up from having to see as many patients, and they were compensated for the value they offered in teaching and coordinating care with other providers.

ProvenCare pathways prioritize work that can be performed with technology.  Whenever possible, work was assigned to trained nonphysician staff so physicians would have more time for direct patient care.  The new care processes were designed to be easier to perform than the previous processes, which helped with their adoption.  

### Scaling and Generalizing ProvenCare Practices

Geisinger focused on scaling and generalizing their care using three platforms.  They scaled their payer/provider capabilities into areas in Pennsylvania and outside Pennsylvania where they had fewer customers or used nonemployed caregiving partners in their hospitals.  They scaled Geisinger insurance into new states – locations where the hospital systems were not owned by Geisinger.  They created xG Health Solutions consulting group as a third scaling platform and for-profit spin-off.

Geisinger had some early successes in scaling to an organization in Virginia and another in rural Illinois.  Overall, their scaling efforts had mixed results.  In some organizations they saw decreases in hospitalizations, readmissions, and ED visits, but the improvements would fade if hospital leadership changed and the new leaders were not as committed to value-based care.  If volume of services decreased from better quality care, hospital leaders in some organizations would increase unit prices.  They also ran into challenges scaling their insurance products, having to compete against dominant local payers.

There were several practices, though, that xG Health Solutions and Geisinger leaders determined were successful within Geisinger and truly scalable to other systems.  Most are listed above in the previous section.  They could implement improvements to the EHR and add bolt-on software, improve data warehousing and analytics, establish consensus-based care pathways integrated into clinical workflows, ensure providers practice at the top of their license, use care management professionals, and provide training and financial incentives for innovation and strategic goals.

### ProvenHealth Navigator

Patients with chronic conditions use the most resources.  Geisinger began embedding nurse care managers in the primary care office as part of a program called ProvenHealth Navigator.  They were employed by the insurance company.  They served as a “concierge caregiver for the sickest patients” (p 127).  The care managers helped manage chronic illness, ensured the patients were safe at home, helped patients take medications as prescribed, assisted in obtaining transportation for appointments, and ensured patients received recommended vaccinations for flu, pneumonia, and shingles.

Having a dedicated care manager helped the care team cut down on unnecessary and wasteful care.  It also helped engage patients and their families in patient health journeys.  More effort and accountability from patients and their families makes a big difference in outcomes.

ProvenHealth Navigator proved successful at reducing inpatient hospital admissions and readmissions.  ProvenHealth Navigator was implemented as part of the ProvenCare Chronic Diabetes program.  The results for the diabetes patient population can also be credited to ProvenHealth Navigator contributions.


### ProvenCare Biologics

Geisinger expanded its bundled best-practice care pathways to include medication prescribing and administration.  They found there were sub-populations of common conditions that were being treated with unnecessarily expensive medications, when a different medication would work just as effectively.  For example, more than 20 percent of chronic renal failure patients with anemia could be treated with oral iron, intravenous iron dextran, or iron sucrose at a fraction of the cost of the typical medications – EPO biologicals.  Some patients, especially older patients, have higher risk of an adverse reaction to EPO treatment and fare better with an iron medication, so the iron alternatives are not only cheaper, but safer for some patients as well.  ProvenCare Biologics introduced a more stringent, standardized set of indications for treatment, resulting in decreased cost and better outcomes.

Another improvement established with the new care pathway was changing the location of medication administration from the nephrologist’s office (in the case of renal failure patients) to a central, less costly location for care, with medications administered by trained staff following best-practice guidelines.  The new protocols for medication administration resulted in less variable and less costly processes and outcomes.

ProvenCare Biologics was also implemented for patients with cancer, psoriasis, hepatitis c, and multiple sclerosis.


### Commentary on Geisinger Innovation

The innovative nature of the care reengineering effort is something of which the authors are very proud.  They cite improved outcomes as evidence of their success.  They cite articles in medical journals and popular media along with mentions by notable people including then-President Barack Obama as evidence of the original and innovative nature of their efforts.  They may overstate the originality of their efforts a bit.  Evidence-based medicine had made great progress in the preceding decades, and the electronic health record provided opportunities for major advances in coordinated care.  Geisinger’s efforts were indeed innovative, but there were other hospitals and groups working on value-based care and population health.  

The authors do at least acknowledge the medical community as the source of evidence-based research that undergirded their care redesign.  Regarding ProvenCare Acute CABG they write:

>Most of the analysis, either evidence-based or consensus-based, of what should be done every time for every patient from the diagnosis of coronary artery disease through rehabilitation had already been processed by the cardiology community and the cardiac surgery disciplines.  What had never been done was a systematic care pathway reprogramming, enabling all of the best practices to be the default for every patient who was diagnosed with coronary artery disease and proposed to be a candidate for CABG.  So ProvenCare Acute really began as a sociology experiment. (p 90)


### Current Quality Improvement Efforts Outside Geisinger

The work of Geisinger and other organizations has changed the way healthcare is provided.  One organization leading the way, now, in value-based care is the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS).  CMS is the biggest purchaser of healthcare in the country and has instituted several programs to reimburse providers based on quality outcomes and cost reduction.  According to the National Association for Healthcare Quality (NAHQ):

>CMS has led the way in launching a number of new models such as the Hospital Value-Based Purchasing (VBP) Program, the Hospital Readmission Reduction Program, and the HAC [Hospital Acquired Condition] Reduction Program, all designed to provide financial incentives for the quality of care delivered.  State Medicaid programs have increasingly included payment reforms in their waivers and Medicaid managed care contracts.  Commercial payers have followed suit and have structured almost one-third of their payments as APMs [Alternative Payment Models].  (NAHQ, p 60)

The CMS value-based models were developed as part of the Medicare Shared Savings Program in 2011, then expanded with the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA) in 2015.  

Other organizations working on improving quality and efficiency include the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA) and the National Quality Forum (NQF).  NCQA produces the Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) measures, which are used to grade health plans and providers on 6 domains of care – effectiveness, access, experience, utilization, electronic clinical data systems, and health plan descriptions.  The NQF reviews quality measures created by other organizations and sets scientific standards for measure development.  CMS and other payers use NQF to review and approve measures.

At Northwestern Medicine, where I currently work, there are continual advances in quality of care.  NM is a top-rated hospital system with nationally recognized specialties.  NM is rated highly by Vizient, Inc., US News and World Report, American Nurses Credentialing Center (ANCC), and other organizations.  Many of Geisinger’s improvement methods are used at NM, including evidence-based practice, embedding best practice alerts and order smart sets into the EHR, designing workflows to function efficiently and cover all necessary aspects of care, delegating work to nurses and support staff where possible to free-up providers to perform research and spend more time with patients, and incorporating quality improvement specialists into multidisciplinary teams.

Some notable performance improvement initiatives related to diabetes care and medication usage are listed below (NM PI Office, p 27, 32):
-	*Developing a Diabetes SmartSet to Promote Guideline-Based Treatment and Ease Prescribing Burden* – An order smart set is an order interface with categories and options related to a patient’s condition.  NM has a diabetes order smart set that is continually evaluated and was recently updated to include supplies such as syringes, needles, and Continuous Glucose Monitors.  Use of the diabetes order smart set has been shown to reduce patient A1c by 10%.  
-	The diabetes EHR workflow was updated in another performance improvement initiative to increase eye exams and screen for retinopathy.  An optometry office was relocated near the physician offices where patients are treated for diabetes, and with the EHR workflow update, eye exams increased significantly.
-	*Assessing the Impact of Rightsizing Controlled Substance (CS) Dosage Forms* – The American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) recommends using the smallest appropriate dose and packaging of controlled substances – “right sizing.”  NM updated the dosages of fentanyl and morphine stocked, replacing most of the 100 mcg and 75 mcg fentanyl packages with 50 mcg and 25 mcg packages and making similar reductions in morphine dosages stocked.  The project resulted in medication waste reductions over 30%.  It also reduced nurse time spent on medication administration.
